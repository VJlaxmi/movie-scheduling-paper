\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

We formulate the MSSP as a multi-objective mixed-integer program over a hierarchical location structure, capturing the full complexity of real-world film production logistics.
Building on our earlier single-objective scheduling work~\citep{vjlaxmi2024}, we substantially extend the constraint model with six new constraint families governing equipment sharing, actor booking contracts, mandatory scene coordination, location concurrency, and lead-actor welfare regulations---yielding a formulation with \textbf{19 constraint families} that addresses operational dimensions absent from prior work~\citep{liu2019,tekin2023}.

\paragraph{Primary Sets.}
Let $\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, \ldots, s_n\}$ be the set of scenes, $\mathcal{A} = \{a_1, \ldots, a_m\}$ the actors, $\mathcal{D} = \{1, \ldots, T\}$ the planning horizon of available shooting days, and $\mathcal{P} \subseteq \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{S}$ the set of precedence pairs.
Let $\mathcal{A}^P \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ denote the subset of \emph{principal (lead) actors} subject to enhanced welfare constraints.

\paragraph{Hierarchical Location Model.}
Real film productions operate across a three-tier geographic hierarchy: \emph{cities} (production bases), \emph{towns} (districts within cities), and \emph{locations} (specific shooting venues within towns).
We model this via three sets and two surjective mappings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \ldots, c_p\}$: cities (production hubs);
    \item $\mathcal{I} = \{i_1, \ldots, i_q\}$: towns, with $\mathcal{I}_c \subseteq \mathcal{I}$ denoting the towns in city $c$;
    \item $\mathcal{L} = \{l_1, \ldots, l_k\}$: locations, with $\mathcal{L}_i \subseteq \mathcal{L}$ denoting the locations in town $i$;
    \item $\phi: \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{I}$: maps each location to its town, $\phi(l) = i \iff l \in \mathcal{L}_i$;
    \item $\psi: \mathcal{I} \to \mathcal{C}$: maps each town to its city, $\psi(i) = c \iff i \in \mathcal{I}_c$.
\end{itemize}
This induces nested containment: $l \in \mathcal{L}_i \Rightarrow i \in \mathcal{I}_{\psi(i)}$, ensuring that activating a location implies its town and city are also active.

\paragraph{Scene Parameters.}
Each scene $s \in \mathcal{S}$ has:
shooting duration $\delta_s$ (hours);
required actors $A(s) \subseteq \mathcal{A}$;
compatible locations $L(s) \subseteq \mathcal{L}$;
optional time window $[e_s, l_s] \subseteq \mathcal{D}$;
optional soft deadline $\bar{d}_s$ with per-day penalty $\rho_s$.

\paragraph{Actor Parameters.}
Each actor $a \in \mathcal{A}$ has:
daily wage $w_a$;
availability calendar $\mathcal{D}_a \subseteq \mathcal{D}$;
criticality weight $\kappa^A_a \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ (reflecting contractual priority);
maximum consecutive working days $\bar{h}_a$ (labor regulations);
booking window $[\beta^s_a, \beta^e_a] \subseteq \mathcal{D}$ (period under production contract);
daily holding fee $h_a \geq 0$ (payment when booked but not shooting);
minimum post-block rest duration $r_a$ (applicable to $a \in \mathcal{A}^P$).

\paragraph{Equipment Parameters.}
Let $\mathcal{E} = \{e_1, \ldots, e_f\}$ be the set of specialized equipment types (cameras, lighting rigs, cranes, etc.).
Each scene $s$ requires equipment subset $E(s) \subseteq \mathcal{E}$, and the production has $\xi_e \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ units of equipment type $e$ available.

\paragraph{Location and Transfer Parameters.}
Each location $l \in \mathcal{L}$ has:
availability calendar $\mathcal{D}_l \subseteq \mathcal{D}$;
criticality weight $\kappa^L_l \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ (reflecting booking difficulty);
concurrency capacity $\mu_l \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ (maximum simultaneous scenes at location $l$ per day).
Transfer costs capture three tiers:
intra-town $\tau^{\text{loc}}_{l,l'}$,
inter-town $\tau^{\text{town}}_{i,i'}$,
inter-city $\tau^{\text{city}}_{c,c'}$.
The composite transfer cost is:
\begin{equation}
c_{l,l'} = \begin{cases}
    \tau^{\text{loc}}_{l,l'} & \text{if } \phi(l) = \phi(l') \\[2pt]
    \tau^{\text{town}}_{\phi(l),\phi(l')} + \tau^{\text{loc}}_{l,l'} & \text{if } \psi(\phi(l)) = \psi(\phi(l')),\; \phi(l) \neq \phi(l') \\[2pt]
    \tau^{\text{city}}_{\psi(\phi(l)),\psi(\phi(l'))} + \tau^{\text{town}}_{\phi(l),\phi(l')} + \tau^{\text{loc}}_{l,l'} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:transfer_cost}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Scene Coordination Parameters.}
Let $\mathcal{G} = \{G_1, \ldots, G_p\}$ be a collection of \emph{mandatory co-scheduling groups}: for each $G_k \in \mathcal{G}$, all scenes in $G_k$ must be shot on the same day (e.g., narrative continuity shots, special-effects setups requiring joint preparation).
Let $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{S}$ be the set of \emph{separated scene pairs} with minimum required inter-scene gaps $\delta_{ij} \geq 1$ (e.g., scenes $(s_i, s_j)$ requiring $\delta_{ij}$ days between them for visual continuity, cast transformation, or location reset).

\paragraph{Binary Indicator Parameters.}
For notational convenience we define:
$b_{a,s} = \mathbb{1}[a \in A(s)]$ (actor $a$ required for scene $s$);
$\alpha_{a,d} = \mathbb{1}[d \in \mathcal{D}_a]$ (actor $a$ available on day $d$);
$\lambda_{l,d} = \mathbb{1}[d \in \mathcal{D}_l]$ (location $l$ available on day $d$);
$p_{s,l} = \mathbb{1}[l \in L(s)]$ (scene $s$ compatible with location $l$);
$\eta_{i,l} = \mathbb{1}[l \in \mathcal{L}_i]$ (location $l$ belongs to town $i$);
$\mu^b_{a,d} = \mathbb{1}[d \in [\beta^s_a, \beta^e_a]]$ (actor $a$ under contract on day $d$);
$\varepsilon_{e,s} = \mathbb{1}[e \in E(s)]$ (scene $s$ requires equipment type $e$).

\paragraph{Decision Variables.}
The model uses seven families of binary decision variables:
\begin{align}
x_{s,d} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{scene } s \text{ scheduled on day } d \label{var:x}\\
y_{s,l} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{scene } s \text{ assigned to location } l \label{var:y}\\
v_{a,s,d} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{actor } a \text{ participates in scene } s \text{ on day } d \label{var:v}\\
z_{l,s,d} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{scene } s \text{ shot at location } l \text{ on day } d \label{var:z}\\
u_{i,d} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{town } i \text{ is the active shooting town on day } d \label{var:u}\\
\phi_{a,d} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{actor } a \text{ is idle (non-shooting) on booked day } d \label{var:phi}\\
w_{a,d} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{actor } a \text{ works (in at least one scene) on day } d \label{var:w}
\end{align}
The linking variables $v_{a,s,d}$ and $z_{l,s,d}$ couple scene scheduling with actor participation and location usage.
The town activation variable $u_{i,d}$ enforces geographic crew logistics.
The booking idle variable $\phi_{a,d}$ captures contracted days on which an actor is not shooting, triggering holding fees in the objective.
The working-day indicator $w_{a,d}$ provides a scene-agnostic view of actor activity, enabling enforcement of lead-actor rest periods across variable-length shooting windows.

\paragraph{Bi-Objective Formulation.}
We minimize two conflicting objectives simultaneously:
\begin{align}
f_1(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{z}, \boldsymbol{\phi}) &= \underbrace{\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} w_a \cdot \bigl|\{d \in \mathcal{D} : \textstyle\sum_{s} v_{a,s,d} \geq 1\}\bigr|}_{\text{(i) actor daily wages}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{d=2}^{T} \sum_{\substack{l, l' \in \mathcal{L} \\ l \neq l'}} c_{l,l'} \cdot \sigma_{l,l',d}}_{\text{(ii) hierarchical transfer costs}} \notag \\[4pt]
&\quad +\; \underbrace{\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \rho_s \cdot \max\bigl(0,\, \textstyle\sum_{d} d \cdot x_{s,d} - \bar{d}_s\bigr)}_{\text{(iii) deadline penalties}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{(l,s,d)} \frac{z_{l,s,d}}{\kappa^L_l} \;+\; \sum_{(a,s,d)} \frac{v_{a,s,d}}{\kappa^A_a}}_{\text{(iv) criticality-weighted utilization}} \notag \\[4pt]
&\quad +\; \underbrace{\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} h_a \cdot \sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} \phi_{a,d}}_{\text{(v) actor holding fees}} \label{eq:cost}\\[8pt]
f_2(\mathbf{x}) &= \max_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \textstyle\sum_d d \cdot x_{s,d} \;-\; \min_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \textstyle\sum_d d \cdot x_{s,d} + 1 \label{eq:makespan}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_{l,l',d} = 1$ if location $l'$ is used on day $d$ and location $l$ was used on day $d{-}1$.
Cost objective $f_1$ has five terms: (i)~actor wages paid per active day, (ii)~hierarchical transfer costs from Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_cost}, (iii)~deadline penalties, (iv)~criticality-weighted utilization penalizing usage of low-criticality resources, and (v)~actor holding fees paid when a contracted actor is not shooting.
Makespan $f_2$ minimizes total production duration.

\paragraph{Constraints.}
The formulation enforces \textbf{19 constraint families} organized into eight categories.
After each group of formal constraints, we provide a brief real-world interpretation to clarify the operational meaning.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{I. Assignment Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
\sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} x_{s,d} &= 1 && \forall s \in \mathcal{S} \tag{C1}\label{c1}\\
\sum_{l \in L(s)} y_{s,l} &= 1 && \forall s \in \mathcal{S} \tag{C2}\label{c2}\\
\text{day}(s_i) + 1 &\leq \text{day}(s_j) && \forall (s_i, s_j) \in \mathcal{P} \tag{C3}\label{c3}\\
e_s \leq \text{day}(s) &\leq l_s && \forall s \text{ with time windows} \tag{C4}\label{c4}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c1}~schedules each scene exactly once---no scene may be split, deferred indefinitely, or duplicated.
\ref{c2}~assigns each scene to exactly one compatible location---a scene cannot be simultaneously staged at multiple venues.
\ref{c3}~enforces narrative or logistic precedence with at least a one-day separation; e.g., a scene set before dawn must precede its sequel.
\ref{c4}~restricts scene scheduling to its permitted shooting window (e.g., seasonal or contractual release windows), where $\text{day}(s) = \sum_{d} d \cdot x_{s,d}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{II. Capacity and Availability Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \delta_s \cdot x_{s,d} &\leq W_{\max} && \forall d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C5}\label{c5}\\
x_{s,d} &\leq \alpha_{a,d} && \forall s \in \mathcal{S},\; a \in A(s),\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C6}\label{c6}\\
z_{l,s,d} &\leq \lambda_{l,d} && \forall l \in L(s),\; s \in \mathcal{S},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C7}\label{c7}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c5}~caps the total shooting duration per calendar day at $W_{\max} = 8$ hours, reflecting union-mandated shooting limits and crew fatigue regulations.
\ref{c6}~prevents scheduling a scene on any day when at least one required actor is unavailable (due to illness, other commitments, or contractual non-availability).
\ref{c7}~ensures locations are used only on days when they are accessible---a location may be closed for maintenance, reserved by other productions, or subject to permit restrictions.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{III. Variable Linking Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
v_{a,s,d} &= x_{s,d} \cdot b_{a,s} \cdot \alpha_{a,d} && \forall a \in \mathcal{A},\; s \in \mathcal{S},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C8}\label{c8}\\
z_{l,s,d} &= x_{s,d} \cdot p_{s,l} \cdot \lambda_{l,d} && \forall l \in \mathcal{L},\; s \in \mathcal{S},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C9}\label{c9}\\
w_{a,d} &\geq v_{a,s,d} && \forall a \in \mathcal{A},\; s \in \mathcal{S},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C9b}\label{c9b}\\
w_{a,d} &\leq \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} v_{a,s,d} && \forall a \in \mathcal{A},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C9c}\label{c9c}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c8}~forces $v_{a,s,d} = 1$ exactly when scene $s$ is on day $d$, actor $a$ is in the cast of $s$, and $a$ is available---providing the fine-grained actor-day participation record needed for wage calculation and labor-limit enforcement.
\ref{c9}~forces $z_{l,s,d} = 1$ exactly when scene $s$ is shot at location $l$ on day $d$ and $l$ is available---enabling transfer cost computation across the three-tier hierarchy.
Since $b_{a,s}$, $\alpha_{a,d}$, $p_{s,l}$, $\lambda_{l,d}$ are fixed binary parameters, C8 and C9 reduce to linear equalities.
\ref{c9b}--\ref{c9c}~together define the \emph{working-day indicator} $w_{a,d}$: $w_{a,d} = 1$ if and only if actor $a$ appears in at least one scene on day $d$.
This scene-agnostic view of actor activity is required by both the consecutive-day labor limit \ref{c13} and the lead-actor rest constraint \ref{c19}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{IV. Geographic Logistics Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
z_{l,s,d} &\leq u_{\phi(l),d} \cdot \eta_{\phi(l),l} \cdot \lambda_{l,d} && \forall l \in L(s),\; s \in \mathcal{S},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C10}\label{c10}\\
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} u_{i,d} &\leq 1 && \forall d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C11}\label{c11}\\
u_{i,d} + u_{j,d-1} &\leq 1 && \forall i, j \in \mathcal{I},\; i \neq j,\; d \geq 2 \tag{C12}\label{c12}\\
\sum_{d'=d}^{\min(d + \bar{h}_a - 1,\, T)} w_{a,d'} &\leq \bar{h}_a && \forall a \in \mathcal{A},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C13}\label{c13}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c10}~enforces location--town consistency: using location $l$ on day $d$ requires its town $\phi(l)$ to be the active town.
\ref{c11}~restricts the crew to \emph{at most one active town per day}---crew and equipment cannot operate in multiple geographic areas simultaneously; all active scenes must be within the same town district.
\ref{c12}~enforces \emph{town continuity across consecutive days}: the crew cannot switch towns between successive shooting days without a dedicated travel day, capturing real-world overnight relocation infeasibility.
\ref{c13}~limits each actor to at most $\bar{h}_a$ \emph{working days} (via the indicator $w_{a,d'}$ from \ref{c9b}--\ref{c9c}) in any rolling window of $\bar{h}_a$ consecutive calendar days, modeling union-mandated limits on consecutive working days and preventing crew burnout.
Using $w_{a,d'}$ rather than $\sum_s v_{a,s,d'}$ is essential: an actor appearing in multiple scenes on the same day counts as one working day, not multiple, correctly tracking the day-level labor constraint.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{V. Equipment Sharing Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \varepsilon_{e,s} \cdot x_{s,d} &\leq \xi_e && \forall e \in \mathcal{E},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C14}\label{c14}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c14}~enforces that on any given day, the total number of scenes simultaneously requiring equipment type $e$ (cranes, underwater rigs, high-speed cameras, etc.) cannot exceed the available supply $\xi_e$.
This models a critical real-world production bottleneck: specialized equipment is expensive to rent and often available in limited quantities, forcing the scheduler to spread equipment-intensive scenes across days.
For standard equipment with $\xi_e \geq n$, this constraint is non-binding and incurs no overhead.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{VI. Actor Booking and Holding Fee Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
\phi_{a,d} &\geq \mu^b_{a,d} - \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} v_{a,s,d} && \forall a \in \mathcal{A},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C15}\label{c15}\\
\phi_{a,d} &\leq \mu^b_{a,d} && \forall a \in \mathcal{A},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C15b}\label{c15b}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c15}~and \ref{c15b}~together force $\phi_{a,d} = 1$ exactly when actor $a$ is under contract on day $d$ (i.e., $\mu^b_{a,d} = 1$) but not shooting any scene.
This models the real-world \emph{pay-or-play} clause in actor contracts: a production that books an actor for a window $[\beta^s_a, \beta^e_a]$ must pay the holding fee $h_a$ for every idle day within that window.
The incentive created is to schedule scenes for contracted actors promptly within their booking windows, avoiding unnecessary idle days and wasted holding payments.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{VII. Scene Coordination Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
x_{s,d} &= x_{s',d} && \forall s, s' \in G_k,\; G_k \in \mathcal{G},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C16}\label{c16}\\
\sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} d \cdot x_{s_j,d} - \sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} d \cdot x_{s_i,d} &\geq \delta_{ij} && \forall (s_i, s_j) \in \mathcal{F} \tag{C17}\label{c17}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c16}~enforces mandatory co-scheduling: all scenes within group $G_k$ must be shot on the same calendar day.
This arises when a single elaborate set or lighting arrangement is used by multiple scenes (e.g., a complex ballroom scene requires the same setup for several shots that cannot be split across days without incurring full re-setup costs), or when scenes share a one-take practical effect.
\ref{c17}~enforces minimum separation between paired scenes: scene $s_j$ must be scheduled at least $\delta_{ij}$ days after scene $s_i$.
Real use cases include: post-production visual-effects windows (an on-set scene must be completed before a VFX plate is shot), actor transformation time (scenes requiring different hair/makeup states), and location reset time (a scene requiring demolition or irreversible set changes before a sequel scene can be filmed).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{VIII. Location Concurrency and Lead-Actor Rest Constraints.}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{align}
\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} z_{l,s,d} &\leq \mu_l && \forall l \in \mathcal{L},\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C18}\label{c18}\\
\sum_{d'=d}^{\min(d + \bar{h}_a + r_a - 1,\, T)} w_{a,d'} &\leq \bar{h}_a && \forall a \in \mathcal{A}^P,\; d \in \mathcal{D} \tag{C19}\label{c19}
\end{align}
\textbf{Interpretation:}
\ref{c18}~limits the number of scenes simultaneously active at location $l$ on any single day to $\mu_l$.
Some locations physically cannot support more than $\mu_l$ simultaneous shooting units due to space constraints, permit conditions, or acoustic interference between adjacent scenes (e.g., $\mu_l = 1$ for a small interior, $\mu_l = 3$ for a large studio with multiple sound stages).
\ref{c19}~enforces a mandatory \emph{rest window} for principal actors: within any rolling window of $\bar{h}_a + r_a$ consecutive days, a lead actor $a \in \mathcal{A}^P$ may work on at most $\bar{h}_a$ days (tracked via the indicator $w_{a,d'}$ from~\ref{c9b}--\ref{c9c}).
This guarantees at least $r_a$ rest days after every consecutive block of $\bar{h}_a$ working days, as required by guild agreements and occupational health regulations for lead performers undertaking physically or psychologically demanding roles.
For lead actors, \ref{c19} strictly subsumes \ref{c13}: the wider window $\bar{h}_a + r_a$ guarantees the mandatory rest margin $r_a$ in addition to the rolling-day limit.

\paragraph{Formulation Complexity.}
The model comprises $O(nmT + nkT + qT + mT)$ binary variables (the additional $mT$ terms from $\phi_{a,d}$ and $w_{a,d}$) and $O(nmT + nkT + q^2 T + |\mathcal{P}| + fT + |\mathcal{G}| \cdot |\mathcal{D}| + |\mathcal{F}| + kT + mT)$ constraints, where $n{=}|\mathcal{S}|$, $m{=}|\mathcal{A}|$, $k{=}|\mathcal{L}|$, $q{=}|\mathcal{I}|$, $f{=}|\mathcal{E}|$, $T{=}|\mathcal{D}|$.
The 19 constraint families (C1--C9c, C10--C19) span eight structural categories, creating dense coupling among scene scheduling, actor availability, equipment sharing, location hierarchy, booking contracts, scene grouping, location concurrency, and labor regulations.
This density makes direct MILP solution intractable for $n > 20$, motivating the decomposition approach in the following subsections.
Compared to Liu~\citep{liu2019} (24 flat constraints) and Tekin~\citep{tekin2023} (31 constraints), our 19 \emph{structured families} capture strictly richer operational semantics through the hierarchical location model, holding-fee accounting, equipment sharing, mandatory co-scheduling, and lead-actor welfare guarantees---aspects absent from all prior formulations.

\subsection{Scene Interaction Graph (SIG)}
\label{sec:sig}

We define an undirected weighted graph $G = (\mathcal{S}, E, w)$ where each scene is a node and edge weights capture structural coupling.
The SIG leverages the hierarchical location model (Section~\ref{sec:formulation}) by computing transfer costs $\bar{c}(s_i, s_j)$ using the composite three-tier formula of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_cost}:
\begin{equation}
w(s_i, s_j) = \alpha \cdot J_A(s_i, s_j) + \beta \cdot J_L(s_i, s_j) + \gamma \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \bar{c}(s_i, s_j)}
\label{eq:sig}
\end{equation}
where $J_A(s_i, s_j) = \frac{|A(s_i) \cap A(s_j)|}{|A(s_i) \cup A(s_j)|}$ is the Jaccard similarity of actor sets, $J_L$ is the Jaccard similarity of location sets, $\bar{c}(s_i, s_j)$ is the average composite transfer cost between compatible locations (reflecting city/town/location distances), and $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$ are weighting parameters (default: $\alpha{=}0.5$, $\beta{=}0.3$, $\gamma{=}0.2$).

Scenes sharing many actors and co-located within the same town receive high edge weights, encoding the intuition that such scenes benefit from consecutive scheduling and naturally satisfy the town-uniqueness constraint~\ref{c11}.
Additionally, scenes within the same mandatory co-scheduling group $G_k \in \mathcal{G}$ receive an artificially high edge weight (set to 1), ensuring Louvain places them in the same block.

\paragraph{Louvain Decomposition.}
We apply Louvain community detection~\citep{blondel2008} to $G$, which maximizes modularity to identify densely connected scene clusters.
The resulting communities form \emph{shooting blocks} $B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_K$, where each block contains tightly coupled scenes.
We iteratively increase the resolution parameter and split blocks exceeding a maximum size to ensure at least $\lceil n/4 \rceil$ blocks, providing sufficient combinatorial richness for the upper-level search.

\subsection{Bi-Level Matheuristic}
\label{sec:bilevel}

\paragraph{Upper Level: NSGA-II over Block Permutations.}
The upper level encodes solutions as permutations of block indices $\pi = (\pi_1, \pi_2, \ldots, \pi_K)$, defining the order in which blocks are scheduled.
This reduces the search space from $n!$ (scene permutations) to $K!$ (block permutations), where typically $K \ll n$.

We use NSGA-II~\citep{deb2002} with population size 20 and 40 generations.
Non-dominated sorting partitions the population into Pareto fronts, and crowding distance maintains solution diversity within each front.

\paragraph{Lower Level: Global Greedy Reassembly.}
For each block permutation $\pi$, we construct a scene-level ordering by concatenating the scenes of each block in sequence: first all scenes from $B_{\pi_1}$ (sorted by constraint tightness), then $B_{\pi_2}$, and so on.
A global greedy decoder then processes this ordering, packing scenes into shooting days while enforcing all 19 constraint families from Section~\ref{sec:formulation}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assignment \& Capacity} (\ref{c1}--\ref{c5}): each scene assigned to exactly one day, respecting capacity ($\leq W_{\max}$ hours/day), precedence, and time windows;
    \item \textbf{Availability} (\ref{c6}--\ref{c7}): actor and location availability calendars are checked before placement;
    \item \textbf{Equipment sharing} (\ref{c14}): equipment counts per day are tracked and not exceeded;
    \item \textbf{Geographic logistics} (\ref{c10}--\ref{c12}): location is chosen to respect town-uniqueness (at most one active town per day) and town-continuity (no town switches on consecutive days), with transfer cost minimized using Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_cost};
    \item \textbf{Actor booking} (\ref{c15}--\ref{c15b}): idle booked days are counted to compute holding fees in the objective;
    \item \textbf{Scene coordination} (\ref{c16}--\ref{c17}): co-scheduling groups are resolved by jointly placing all member scenes; separation constraints are enforced as additional day-assignment bounds;
    \item \textbf{Location concurrency and labor} (\ref{c18}, \ref{c19}): location concurrency limits and lead-actor rest windows are checked before placement;
    \item Days are shared across blocks naturally, avoiding the cost overhead of isolated per-block scheduling.
\end{itemize}

This \emph{global reassembly} approach ensures that the decomposition guides the ordering while the greedy decoder handles day packing with full constraint enforcement, achieving cost-competitive solutions while preserving the search-space reduction of block-level evolution.

\paragraph{Solution Evaluation.}
Each chromosome $\pi$ is evaluated by the global greedy reassembly: scenes are ordered according to the block permutation, then greedily assigned to days.
Evaluation results are cached (keyed by $\pi$) to avoid redundant computation.
The result is a bi-objective vector $(f_1, f_2)$ fed to NSGA-II.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{BDM-AOS: Bi-Level Decomposition Matheuristic}
\label{alg:bdm_aos}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE MSSP instance $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{I}, \mathcal{L}, \mathcal{D}, \phi, \psi)$, parameters $(N, G, p_c, p_m)$
\ENSURE Pareto front of schedules
\STATE Build Scene Interaction Graph $G_{\text{SIG}}$ via Eq.~(\ref{eq:sig})
\STATE $\{B_1, \ldots, B_K\} \leftarrow$ Louvain$(G_{\text{SIG}})$
\STATE Initialize population $P = \{\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_N\}$ (random block permutations)
\STATE Evaluate each $\pi_i$ via global greedy reassembly
\FOR{$g = 1$ to $G$}
    \STATE Compute diversity $d$, improvement rate $r$, quality gap $q$
    \STATE $(cx, mut) \leftarrow$ AOS.select$(g, G, d, r, q)$ \COMMENT{Q-learning}
    \STATE Generate offspring via $cx$ crossover and $mut$ mutation
    \STATE Evaluate offspring via bi-level solving
    \STATE $P \leftarrow$ NSGA-II environmental selection$(P \cup \text{offspring})$
    \STATE AOS.update(reward $= \Delta$HV)
\ENDFOR
\RETURN Non-dominated solutions from $P$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Q-Learning Adaptive Operator Selection}
\label{sec:aos}

We formulate operator selection as a reinforcement learning problem.

\paragraph{State Space.}
The state is discretized from four continuous features:
(i)~generation progress (early/mid/late),
(ii)~population diversity via normalized Hamming distance (low/medium/high),
(iii)~improvement rate of hypervolume over the last generation (stagnant/slow/fast),
(iv)~quality gap from the reference point (good/moderate/poor).
This yields $3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 81$ possible states.

\paragraph{Action Space.}
Each action selects a (crossover, mutation) pair from $\{$PMX, OX, LAX$\} \times \{$swap, block-swap, ACM$\}$, giving 9 actions.

\paragraph{Reward.}
The reward after applying action $a$ in state $s$ is the change in hypervolume:
$r = \text{HV}(P_{g+1}) - \text{HV}(P_g)$.

\paragraph{Update Rule.}
Standard Q-learning with $\epsilon$-greedy exploration ($\epsilon_0 = 0.3$, decay $0.995$, minimum $0.05$):
\begin{equation}
Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha \left[ r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a) \right]
\label{eq:qlearning}
\end{equation}
with learning rate $\alpha = 0.1$ and discount factor $\gamma = 0.9$.

\subsection{Novel Problem-Specific Operators}
\label{sec:novel_ops}

\paragraph{Location-Aware Crossover (LAX).}
LAX preserves \emph{location runs}: maximal sub-sequences of blocks sharing the same primary location in a parent.
The longest location run from parent 1 is copied intact to the child, and remaining positions are filled from parent 2 in order.
This preserves spatial locality, reducing inter-block transfer costs.

\paragraph{Actor-Continuity Mutation (ACM).}
ACM identifies the actor with the largest idle gap between block appearances.
It moves the later block immediately after the earlier one, reducing the number of idle days (and thus wage payments and holding fees) for that actor.
This directly exploits the MSSP cost structure where actor wages and holding fees dominate total cost.

\subsection{Computational Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}

\paragraph{Problem Complexity.}
The MSSP is NP-hard by reduction from the single-machine weighted completion time problem~\citep{garey1979, pinedo2016}.
Even the single-objective variant (minimizing cost only) requires searching over $n!$ scene orderings subject to the 19 constraint families---including variable linking (\ref{c8}--\ref{c9c}), geographic logistics (\ref{c10}--\ref{c12}), actor labor limits (\ref{c13}), equipment sharing (\ref{c14}), actor booking (\ref{c15}--\ref{c15b}), scene coordination (\ref{c16}--\ref{c17}), and location concurrency and lead-actor rest (\ref{c18}--\ref{c19})---that create dense variable coupling with $O(nmT + nkT + qT + mT)$ binary variables.

\paragraph{Search Space Reduction.}
BDM-AOS exploits the SIG structure to decompose $n$ scenes into $K$ blocks, reducing the upper-level search space from $O(n!)$ to $O(K!)$.
For our largest instance (S100: $n=100$ scenes), Louvain decomposition yields $K \approx 24$ blocks, reducing the search space by a factor exceeding $\frac{100!}{24!} \approx 10^{134}$.
Even at moderate scale (S50: $n=50$, $K \approx 12$), the reduction factor is $\frac{50!}{12!} \approx 10^{55}$.

\paragraph{Per-Generation Complexity.}
Each generation of the NSGA-II upper level involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Crossover/mutation}: $O(N \cdot K)$ per offspring, where $N$ is population size and $K$ is the number of blocks;
    \item \textit{Greedy reassembly decoding}: $O(n \cdot T)$ per individual, where $T$ is the planning horizon;
    \item \textit{Non-dominated sorting}: $O(N^2 \cdot 2)$ for bi-objective problems;
    \item \textit{AOS Q-table lookup}: $O(1)$ (81-state, 9-action table).
\end{itemize}

The total per-generation cost is $O(N \cdot (n \cdot T + K))$, dominated by the greedy decoder evaluations.

\paragraph{Overall Complexity.}
With $G$ generations and population size $N$:
$\text{Total} = O(G \cdot N \cdot n \cdot T)$.
Using our default parameters ($N=20$, $G=40$), this is $O(800 \cdot n \cdot T)$, linear in instance size for fixed parameters---a significant advantage over exact methods whose worst-case complexity is exponential.
