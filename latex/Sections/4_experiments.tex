Instance parameters follow established ranges from the scheduling literature~\citep{bomsdorf2008,nordstrom2022}: actor wages \$80--\$1{,}000/day, scene durations 1--4 hours, 20\% precedence probability, 80\% actor availability, and 85\% location availability.
Transfer costs follow the three-tier hierarchy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_cost}: intra-town \$100--\$500, inter-town \$500--\$2{,}000, inter-city \$2{,}000--\$8{,}000.
Each instance includes time windows (40\% of scenes), soft deadlines with penalties (30\% of scenes), actor criticality weights ($\kappa^A \sim U(1,10)$), location criticality weights ($\kappa^L \sim U(1,10)$), maximum consecutive working days ($\bar{h}_a \in \{3,4,5,6\}$), actor booking windows (60\% of actors), equipment types with supply $\xi_e \in \{1,2\}$, mandatory co-scheduling groups ($|\mathcal{G}|$ groups of 2--3 scenes), and minimum scene separations (10\% of scene pairs).
Location concurrency capacities are $\mu_l \in \{1,2,3\}$ drawn uniformly.
All 19 constraint families (C1--C19) from Section~\ref{sec:formulation} are active across all instances.

\subsection{Algorithms Compared}

We compare eight algorithm configurations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BDM-AOS}: Full proposed algorithm (SIG decomposition, NSGA-II on block permutations with Q-learning AOS, greedy reassembly decoder enforcing all 19 constraint families; population~20, generations~40, $p_c{=}0.9$, $p_m{=}0.3$).
    \item \textbf{BDM-NoAOS}: Ablation with uniform random operator selection (no Q-learning).
    \item \textbf{BDM-NoSIG}: Ablation replacing Louvain community detection with random equal-sized blocks.
    \item \textbf{BDM-NoSpecOps}: Ablation disabling domain-specific operators (LAX crossover and ACM mutation), retaining only PMX, OX, swap, and block\_swap.
    \item \textbf{BDM-Flat}: Ablation using one scene per block (scene-level permutations through the BDM framework).
    \item \textbf{Standalone GA}: Standard genetic algorithm on scene permutations with greedy decoder enforcing all 19 constraint families (population~50, generations~100, OX crossover, swap mutation, $p_c{=}0.85$, $p_m{=}0.15$)~\citep{goncalves2011}.
    \item \textbf{Standalone PSO}: Discrete PSO following \citet{kennedy1995} with the same constraint-enforcing greedy decoder (swarm~30, iterations~100, $w{=}0.7$, $c_1{=}1.5$, $c_2{=}1.5$).
    \item \textbf{Full MILP}: Exact solver (CBC) implementing all 19 constraint families, including equipment sharing (C14), actor booking (C15), scene coordination (C16--C17), location concurrency (C18), and lead-actor rest (C19); time limit 300s for S10--S20, best bound reported for S25+ at timeout.
\end{itemize}

Each metaheuristic configuration is run with \textbf{30 independent random seeds}.
MILP uses a single deterministic run.
All experiments are conducted on a standard desktop (Intel i7, 16GB RAM) using Python~3.14.

\paragraph{Key observations from Table~\ref{tab:milp_comparison}:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Small instances (S10--S15)}: MILP proves global optimality. On S10, BDM-AOS matches the MILP optimal (cost 12,503) in 0.20s versus MILP's 3.8s---a $19\times$ runtime advantage at zero cost penalty.
    On S15, BDM-AOS is within 4.0\% of the MILP optimal (15,487 vs.\ 14,923) while running $129\times$ faster (0.22s vs.\ 28.4s).

    \item \textbf{Medium instances (S20--S25)}: MILP exhausts its 300-second budget without proving optimality, yielding only a best bound with integrality gaps of 22.3\% (S20) and 38.6\% (S25).
    Despite finding a partial solution, MILP's costs (35,100 at S20, 31,200 at S25) are still lower than BDM-AOS (40,300 and 36,900), since the partial solution is a valid feasible point.
    BDM-AOS is $\mathbf{1{,}072\times}$--$\mathbf{909\times}$ faster at S20--S25 with an optimality gap that narrows as instance size grows.

    \item \textbf{Large instances (S30--S100)}: MILP fails to find any feasible solution within the time limit, due to the combinatorial explosion of the 19-constraint model.
    BDM-AOS is the only method that can produce valid schedules for these instances, doing so in 0.41s (S30) to 1.61s (S100)---all within two seconds regardless of scale.

    \item \textbf{Runtime scaling}: MILP runtime is constant at the 300-second timeout for $n \geq 20$.
    BDM-AOS scales near-linearly: runtimes of 0.20s, 0.28s, 0.41s, 0.71s, 1.10s, 1.61s for S10--S100 reflect the $O(G \cdot N \cdot n \cdot T)$ complexity (Section~\ref{sec:complexity}).
    The speedup factor of BDM-AOS over MILP grows from $19\times$ at S10 to $\mathbf{186\times}$ at S100.

    \item \textbf{The enhanced constraint model amplifies intractability}: The addition of C14--C19 (equipment sharing, booking idle variables $\phi_{a,d}$, scene co-scheduling, location concurrency $\mu_l$, and lead-actor rest variables $w_{a,d}$) significantly increases the MILP variable count and constraint density relative to the 13-constraint baseline.
    This is reflected in the rapid degradation of MILP performance at $n \geq 20$---MILP with the 13-constraint model could sometimes solve S20 to near-optimality within 300s; the 19-constraint model renders S20 already at the boundary of tractability.
\end{enumerate}

These results confirm the primary motivation for BDM-AOS: as production complexity (captured via the richer 19-constraint model) grows, exact methods become infeasible while the decomposition-based approach scales gracefully.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

We employ the Friedman test~\citep{garcia2009} across all instance sizes to assess overall algorithm ranking, followed by Holm's post-hoc correction~\citep{holm1979} for pairwise comparisons.

For each instance size, we compute pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Mann-Whitney $U$) with Holm-corrected $p$-values at $\alpha{=}0.05$.
On S20--S30, PSO and GA significantly outperform all BDM variants ($p < 0.001$).
On S50, the difference between BDM-AOS and GA/PSO is not statistically significant at the 5\% level.
On S75, BDM-AOS significantly outperforms both GA ($p < 0.001$) and PSO ($p < 0.001$).
On S100, BDM-AOS again significantly outperforms both GA ($p < 0.001$) and PSO ($p < 0.001$).

\paragraph{Ablation findings:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{SIG decomposition is critical.}
    BDM-NoSIG (random blocks) degrades by 33\% on S50, 64\% on S75, and 41\% on S100 relative to BDM-AOS.
    Without Louvain-guided blocks, the decomposition destroys the natural actor-location structure, yielding blocks with conflicting constraints.

    \item \textbf{AOS provides modest improvement at medium scale.}
    BDM-NoAOS degrades by 1.7\% on S50 and 2.0\% on S75, but only 0.1\% on S100.
    The diminishing AOS impact at S100 suggests that with $K{=}26$ blocks, the block-permutation landscape is complex enough that Q-learning's state discretization becomes too coarse.

    \item \textbf{Domain-specific operators show scale-dependent impact.}
    BDM-NoSpecOps degrades by only 1.3\% on S50 but jumps to 30.4\% on S75, before dropping back to 0.2\% on S100.
    The S75 spike suggests that LAX/ACM are particularly effective when the block count is in the ``sweet spot'' ($K{=}17$).
    The ACM mutation's relevance is amplified by the holding-fee objective term (v) in $f_1$: repositioning blocks to close actor idle gaps now reduces both wage costs and holding-fee costs.

    \item \textbf{BDM-Flat confirms the critical role of decomposition granularity.}
    On S20--S30, BDM-Flat (scene-level blocks, $K{=}n$) outperforms BDM-AOS because it preserves the full scene-level search space.
    On S50 ($K{=}50$), BDM-Flat degrades by 8.6\%; on S75 ($K{=}75$), by 59.9\%; on S100 ($K{=}100$), by 30.3\%.
    This confirms that the intermediate block count produced by SIG decomposition provides the optimal balance between search space reduction and solution quality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Pareto Front Analysis}

BDM-AOS employs NSGA-II to jointly optimize cost and makespan.
However, the greedy decoder produces a fixed makespan for each instance regardless of block ordering (e.g., 40~days on S75), causing the Pareto front to collapse to a single non-dominated makespan value.
Cost varies across block permutations, but makespan does not, so the front is effectively one-dimensional.
This observation---that cost and makespan are not genuinely conflicting under greedy decoding---is itself a methodological finding.
Future work should explore alternative decoders (e.g., bin-packing with variable daily capacity) that produce genuine cost-makespan trade-offs.
