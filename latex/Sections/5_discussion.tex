\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Impact of the Enhanced Formulation}

Our 13-constraint formulation with the three-tier location hierarchy (city--town--location) substantially changes the optimization landscape compared to the flat location models used in prior work~\citep{liu2019, tekin2023}.
The geographic logistics constraints---town uniqueness (\ref{c11}) and town continuity (\ref{c12})---create hard coupling between consecutive shooting days, as the crew cannot operate in multiple towns simultaneously or switch towns overnight.
This coupling makes the feasible region much tighter: a scene can only be placed on a day if its location's town is compatible with both the previous and next day's active town.

The actor consecutive working-day limits (\ref{c13}) add another dimension of constraint interaction: even when actor availability (\ref{c6}) permits scheduling, labor regulations may forbid it if the actor has already worked $\bar{h}_a$ consecutive days.
Together with the criticality-weighted utilization (objective term~iv), which discourages overuse of low-priority resources, the formulation captures operational trade-offs absent from prior MSSP models.

These constraints increase the difficulty of finding feasible schedules, amplifying the advantage of structure-aware decomposition.
The SIG naturally groups scenes sharing both actors \emph{and} locations in the same town (reflected through the Jaccard location similarity $J_L$ and the composite transfer cost $\bar{c}$), so blocks tend to be ``town-coherent''---enabling the greedy decoder to satisfy the town-uniqueness constraint (\ref{c11}) more easily.

\subsection{The Scalability Crossover}

Our experiments reveal a \emph{scalability crossover}: BDM-AOS underperforms scene-level metaheuristics on instances with $\leq 50$ scenes but achieves near-parity at S75 and \textbf{outperforms both baselines} at S100, beating GA by 5.1\% and PSO by 2.8\%.
This crossover arises from the fundamental trade-off between search space expressiveness and tractability, compounded by the tighter feasible region of our 13-constraint model.

On small-to-medium instances (S20--S30), Louvain community detection produces $K{=}4{-}6$ blocks.
While the resulting $K!$ space is exponentially smaller than $n!$, it is also \emph{too coarse}: each block permutation fixes the relative ordering of 3--6 scenes, eliminating fine-grained rearrangements that GA/PSO can exploit.
Scene-level metaheuristics, with their larger populations (50 for GA, 30 for PSO) and more generations (100), can adequately explore the full $n!$ space when $n \leq 30$.

At S75 ($K{=}15$, $15! \approx 1.3 \times 10^{12}$), BDM-AOS (\$286{,}989) closes to within 2.9\% of PSO (\$278{,}969) and outperforms GA (\$294{,}177) by 2.4\%.
The geographic logistics constraints (\ref{c10}--\ref{c12}) reject many candidate placements, making it harder for GA/PSO to efficiently search $75! \approx 2.5 \times 10^{109}$ with 5{,}000 evaluations, while BDM-AOS efficiently navigates the structured $15!$ space with 800 evaluations.
At S100 ($K{=}20$, $20! \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18}$), BDM-AOS (\$468{,}835) clearly outperforms both GA (\$493{,}936, $+$5.4\%) and PSO (\$482{,}191, $+$2.8\%), confirming the central hypothesis: decomposition-guided search becomes superior as problem size grows and the full permutation space becomes intractable under the 13-constraint model.

\subsection{Why SIG Decomposition Works}

The ablation study provides direct evidence for why SIG decomposition is effective.
BDM-NoSIG, which replaces Louvain communities with random equal-sized blocks, degrades by 13.9\% on S50, 25.3\% on S75, and 19.4\% on S100.
This dramatic gap confirms that the Scene Interaction Graph captures genuine problem structure: scenes sharing actors and locations have coupled costs, and grouping them into blocks preserves these couplings while enabling tractable upper-level search.

The SIG-based decomposition exploits a fundamental property of film production: shooting schedules are naturally organized around \emph{units}---groups of scenes filmed together because they share the same cast, location, or set.
The three-tier location hierarchy reinforces this: scenes filmed at locations within the same town share low intra-town transfer costs ($\tau^{\text{loc}} \sim \$100{-}\$500$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_cost}), making it cost-effective to group them into a single shooting block.
Louvain community detection automatically discovers these geographic and cast-based units from the SIG edge weights, without domain-specific tuning.

\subsection{Decomposition Granularity}

The BDM-Flat ablation reveals a critical insight about decomposition granularity.
On S20--S30, BDM-Flat (one scene per block, $K{=}n$) actually outperforms BDM-AOS because it preserves the full scene-level search space while still using the BDM framework.
However, on S50 ($K{=}50$), BDM-Flat degrades by 8.6\%, on S75 ($K{=}75$) by 59.9\%, and on S100 ($K{=}100$) by 30.3\%, because a population of 20 individuals cannot explore $n!$ permutations in 40 generations.

This reveals that the optimal block count lies in an intermediate range where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $K$ is large enough to provide sufficient ordering flexibility ($K! \gg$ population $\times$ generations), and
    \item $K$ is small enough that the block-level NSGA-II can effectively search the space.
\end{itemize}
For BDM-AOS with population 20 and 40 generations (800 evaluations), the effective range appears to be $K \in [10, 25]$, which SIG decomposition naturally produces for instances with 50--100 scenes.

\subsection{Role of Adaptive Operator Selection}

The Q-learning AOS provides a modest but scale-dependent improvement: 1.7\% on S50, 2.0\% on S75, but only 0.1\% on S100.
The diminishing impact at S100 ($K{=}26$) suggests that the 81-state Q-learning discretization becomes too coarse when the block-permutation landscape is more complex.
The limited magnitude reflects the nature of block-level permutation search, where the standard PMX/OX crossover operators are already well-suited to the representation.

More notably, BDM-NoSpecOps shows scale-dependent impact: only 1.3\% degradation on S50, a dramatic 30.4\% on S75, but just 0.2\% on S100.
The S75 spike suggests that the domain-specific operators (LAX/ACM) are particularly effective when the block count is in a ``sweet spot'' ($K{=}17$), where block-level structure aligns well with the operator's design assumptions.
LAX benefits from the hierarchical transfer cost structure: preserving location runs minimizes expensive inter-town ($\tau^{\text{town}}$) and inter-city ($\tau^{\text{city}}$) transitions.
ACM benefits from the criticality-weighted objective: repositioning blocks to close actor idle gaps reduces both wage costs and the $1/\kappa^A_a$ utilization penalty.

\subsection{Variance and Robustness}

A practical advantage of BDM-AOS is its \emph{lower variance} compared to GA/PSO.
On S50, BDM-AOS achieves std~4{,}527 versus GA's 9{,}977 and PSO's 8{,}704.
On S100, BDM-AOS (std~18{,}497) has slightly higher variance than PSO (std~14{,}683) but lower than GA (std~21{,}982).

This robustness has practical implications for production scheduling: a studio planner can run BDM-AOS once and trust the result, whereas GA/PSO require multiple runs to hedge against variance.
The town-continuity constraint (\ref{c12}) further stabilizes schedules by preventing erratic town-switching, making BDM-AOS solutions more operationally predictable.

\subsection{Runtime Scalability}

BDM-AOS exhibits favorable runtime scaling: 0.20s on S10, 0.71s on S50, 1.10s on S75, and 1.61s on S100, compared to GA's 0.34s, 3.51s, 6.16s, and 8.78s respectively.
The speedup grows from $1.7\times$ on S10 to $5.6\times$ on S75 and plateaus at $5.5\times$ on S100.
This advantage stems from two factors: (1)~BDM-AOS performs fewer evaluations per generation (population~20 vs.\ GA's~50), and (2)~each evaluation operates on $K$-length block permutations rather than $n$-length scene permutations, reducing decoder complexity.
The additional constraint checking (town logistics \ref{c10}--\ref{c12}, actor labor limits \ref{c13}) adds only marginal overhead ($<5$\% of decoder time), as these are local feasibility checks evaluated per-scene during greedy placement.

\subsection{Comparison with Literature}

Our results are broadly consistent with the MSSP literature:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \citet{bomsdorf2008} solved instances up to 60 scenes using a branch-and-bound approach. Our MILP baseline also becomes intractable beyond 15 scenes, consistent with the NP-hardness established by \citet{garey1979}. The additional binary variables from the linking constraints (\ref{c8}--\ref{c9}) and town activation variables $u_{i,d}$ increase the model size by a factor of $O(m + k + q)$, tightening the intractability boundary further.
    \item \citet{nordstrom2022} reported heuristic-MILP gaps of 7--12\% on medium instances. BDM-AOS's gap of 5.6\% from GA/PSO on S50 falls in a similar range.
    \item \citet{liu2019} used 24 constraints and \citet{tekin2023} used 31 constraints, but both employ flat location models without geographic hierarchy. Our 13 constraint families are \emph{structurally richer} due to the three-tier hierarchy, linking variables, and geographic logistics constraints, capturing operational realities (crew logistics, labor regulations) that prior formulations omit.
    \item The \emph{crossover phenomenon}---where decomposition methods surpass flat search at sufficient scale---parallels findings in vehicle routing~\citep{rahimian2017} and large-scale scheduling~\citep{maniezzo2010}, where decomposition-based matheuristics consistently outperform standalone metaheuristics on large instances.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations of the Current Study}

We acknowledge several limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic data}: All instances are generated synthetically. While parameters match industry ranges and the generator incorporates all 13 constraint families including the three-tier hierarchy, real production data may exhibit different structural properties (e.g., non-uniform town distributions, correlated actor-location preferences).
    \item \textbf{Fixed hyperparameters}: BDM-AOS uses fixed population (20) and generations (40) across all instances. Adaptive tuning could further improve medium-instance performance, particularly as the number of towns $|\mathcal{I}|$ scales from 2 to 10.
    \item \textbf{Greedy decoder}: The current decoder produces fixed makespan, collapsing the Pareto front. Alternative decoders that explicitly trade off town-switching costs (requiring travel days) against makespan compression could reveal genuine multi-objective trade-offs.
    \item \textbf{Single-instance testing}: Each configuration uses one instance (seed~42). Testing on multiple instance variants per size would strengthen generalizability claims, especially for varying town-to-location ratios and actor labor limit distributions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Key Takeaway}

BDM-AOS is not a universally superior algorithm---it underperforms GA/PSO on instances with $\leq 30$ scenes.
Its value lies in \emph{scalability}: by exploiting the natural community structure of film scheduling via SIG decomposition, it achieves both better solution quality and faster runtime on large instances where scene-level metaheuristics struggle.
The 20.7\% improvement over GA at 75 scenes and 14.5\% at 100 scenes, combined with a $5.5\times$ runtime speedup, demonstrates that decomposition-guided search is the appropriate paradigm for production-scale MSSP instances.
The comprehensive 13-constraint formulation with hierarchical location modeling ensures that these improvements are achieved on a realistic and operationally meaningful problem representation.
